## Backpropagation

Backpropagation is the algorithm that computes the gradient of the loss function with respect to every parameter of a neural network. It applies the chain rule of calculus in reverse, walking from the output layer back toward the input. The network is treated as a computational graph: each node computes a simple function of its inputs, and each edge carries an intermediate value.

Training alternates two passes. The forward pass feeds an input through the network, computes the activations of every layer and the final loss, and stores those activations. The backward pass starts from the derivative of the loss with respect to the output and, layer by layer, multiplies by the local derivative of each operation. For a layer computing z = Wa + b followed by a nonlinearity, the gradient with respect to W is the outer product of the upstream gradient and the stored input activation a, and the gradient passed further back is W transposed times the upstream gradient.

Because the backward pass reuses the activations stored during the forward pass, no partial derivative is computed twice. The total cost of computing all gradients is on the same order as one forward pass, usually quoted as two to three times its cost, no matter how many parameters the network has. Estimating each partial derivative separately by finite differences would instead need an extra forward pass per parameter, which is hopeless for networks with millions of weights. This efficiency is what makes training deep networks tractable.

Backpropagation only computes gradients; an optimizer such as stochastic gradient descent then uses them to update the weights. Modern frameworks implement the idea as reverse-mode automatic differentiation, recording operations on a tape during the forward pass. Memory is the main price: the activations of every layer must be kept until the backward pass consumes them, which is why gradient checkpointing trades extra computation for lower memory. Deep stacks of saturating activations can also make gradients shrink exponentially as they flow backward, the vanishing gradient problem discussed in the section on activation functions.

## Overfitting and Regularisation

Overfitting occurs when a model captures noise specific to the training set rather than the underlying signal. An overfit model has effectively memorised its training examples: it scores very well on the data it was fitted to and noticeably worse on data it has never seen.

The classic signs appear in the learning curves. Training loss continues to decrease while validation loss stops improving and then begins to rise, and the gap between training and held-out performance keeps widening as training goes on. A model with far more free parameters than training examples, such as a high-degree polynomial fitted to a dozen points, is the textbook case: the curve passes through every point but oscillates wildly between them.

Common remedies limit either the model's capacity or how much it can extract from noise. Regularisation adds a penalty to the loss. L2 regularisation, also called weight decay, adds the sum of squared weights and shrinks all weights smoothly toward zero, while L1 regularisation adds the sum of absolute values and drives many weights exactly to zero, producing sparse models. Dropout randomly zeroes a fraction of the units, often half of them, on each training step, so units cannot rely on specific partners and are forced to learn redundant, robust features; at test time all units are kept and activations are scaled to match. Early stopping monitors validation loss and halts training, restoring the best checkpoint, once it has not improved for a set number of epochs. Data augmentation enlarges the effective training set with label-preserving transformations such as crops, flips and small rotations of images. Finally, simply reducing model capacity, with fewer layers, fewer units or a lower polynomial degree, removes the ability to memorise.

The opposite failure, underfitting, shows up as high error on both training and validation data. It is fixed by adding capacity or training longer rather than by regularising. Telling the two apart always requires a held-out set that the model never trains on.

## The Bias-Variance Tradeoff

The bias-variance decomposition explains why both overly simple and overly flexible models generalise poorly. Consider squared-error regression and imagine retraining the same learning algorithm on many different training sets drawn from the same distribution. For a fixed test input, the total expected error decomposes into three terms: bias squared, variance, and irreducible noise.

Bias measures how far the average prediction, taken over all those training sets, lies from the true value. It comes from wrong assumptions built into the model, for example fitting a straight line to a curved relationship. Variance measures how much the predictions fluctuate around their own average as the training set changes; it comes from sensitivity to the particular sample. The noise term is the variance of the target itself given the input. No model can remove it, so it sets a floor on the achievable error.

High-bias models underfit: a linear model on a strongly nonlinear problem makes similar mistakes whichever sample it sees. High-variance models overfit: a deep, unpruned decision tree changes shape dramatically when a few training points change. Increasing model capacity typically lowers bias and raises variance, so a plot of test error against capacity is often U-shaped, and the goal is to find the sweet spot for the given dataset size. More training data moves the sweet spot toward more complex models, because variance shrinks as the sample grows while bias does not.

Several standard techniques are best understood through this lens. Regularisation deliberately accepts a little extra bias in exchange for a large reduction in variance. Bagging, which averages models trained on bootstrap resamples, reduces variance while leaving bias roughly unchanged. Boosting, which adds weak learners one after another, mainly reduces bias. Very large neural networks complicate the classic picture: beyond the point where they can fit the training data exactly, test error can fall again, a phenomenon called double descent. The decomposition itself still holds for any fixed model class.

## Activation Functions

An activation function is the nonlinearity applied to each unit's weighted input. Without it, a stack of linear layers collapses into a single linear map, so depth would add no expressive power.

The logistic sigmoid, 1 / (1 + e^-x), squashes its input into the range 0 to 1 and was the standard choice in early networks. Its derivative never exceeds 0.25 and approaches zero when the input is large in magnitude, so the unit saturates. The hyperbolic tangent, tanh, has the same S shape but is centred on zero, with outputs between -1 and 1, which usually makes optimisation easier than with the sigmoid; it still saturates at both ends.

Saturation causes the vanishing gradient problem. During backpropagation the gradient that reaches an early layer is a product of many local derivatives. When each factor is well below one, the product shrinks exponentially with depth and the early layers barely learn.

The rectified linear unit, ReLU, computes max(0, x). Its gradient is exactly 1 for positive inputs and 0 for negative ones, so it does not saturate on the positive side, it is very cheap to evaluate, and it produces sparse activations. ReLU made it practical to train much deeper networks and remains the default for hidden layers. Its weakness is the dying ReLU problem: a unit whose input becomes negative for every example outputs zero, receives zero gradient and may never recover, which is more likely with a large learning rate. Leaky ReLU fixes this with a small slope, such as 0.01, for negative inputs, while ELU and GELU are smooth variants; GELU is the usual choice inside transformers.

Output layers use activations matched to the task. Softmax turns a vector of scores into a probability distribution over classes and is paired with the cross-entropy loss; a single sigmoid output suits binary classification; regression usually has no output activation at all. Careful weight initialisation, such as He initialisation for ReLU networks, keeps activations and gradients at a stable scale from layer to layer.

## Gradient Descent and Optimizers

Gradient descent minimises a loss by repeatedly stepping the parameters in the direction of the negative gradient: theta becomes theta minus eta times the gradient, where eta is the learning rate. Full-batch gradient descent computes the exact gradient over the whole training set before every step, which is accurate but expensive on large datasets.

Stochastic gradient descent, SGD, estimates the gradient from a single example or, in practice, from a mini-batch of typically 32 to 512 examples. Each step is cheap and noisy. The noise helps the optimizer escape sharp minima and saddle points, and mini-batches map efficiently onto GPU hardware. One pass over the training data is called an epoch.

The learning rate is the most important hyperparameter. If it is too large the loss oscillates or diverges; if it is too small training crawls or stalls on a plateau. Schedules change it over time: step decay divides it by a constant every few epochs, cosine annealing lowers it smoothly toward zero, and a short warmup that raises it linearly at the start stabilises the training of large models.

Momentum keeps an exponentially decaying average of past gradients, commonly with coefficient 0.9, and steps along that velocity instead of the raw gradient. It accelerates progress along directions where successive gradients agree and damps oscillation across narrow valleys. Nesterov momentum evaluates the gradient at the look-ahead position and often converges slightly faster.

Adaptive methods give every parameter its own effective learning rate. AdaGrad divides by the square root of the accumulated sum of squared gradients, which suits sparse features but lets the step size shrink toward zero. RMSProp replaces that sum with an exponential moving average. Adam combines momentum with RMSProp-style scaling: it tracks the first moment, the mean, and the second moment, the uncentred variance, of the gradients, with typical decay rates beta1 = 0.9 and beta2 = 0.999, and applies a bias correction because both averages start at zero. AdamW decouples weight decay from the gradient update and is the common default for transformers, while well-tuned SGD with momentum remains competitive for convolutional networks.

## Model Evaluation and Cross-Validation

A model must be judged on data it was not trained on. The standard protocol splits the data three ways: a training set to fit the parameters, a validation set to choose hyperparameters and compare models, and a test set that is used once, at the very end, to estimate performance on future data. Reusing the test set for model selection quietly turns it into another validation set and makes the final estimate optimistic.

When data is scarce, k-fold cross-validation makes better use of it. The data is partitioned into k folds of equal size, commonly k = 5 or 10. The model is trained k times, each time holding out a different fold for evaluation and training on the remaining k - 1 folds, and the k scores are averaged, so every example is used for validation exactly once. Stratified k-fold keeps the class proportions the same in every fold, which matters for imbalanced labels, and leave-one-out cross-validation is the extreme case in which k equals the number of examples.

Data leakage is the most common way evaluations go wrong. Leakage happens when information from the validation or test data influences training, for example when features are normalised with statistics computed on the full dataset before splitting, or when records from the same patient appear on both sides of the split. For time series, folds must respect time order so that the model never trains on the future.

Accuracy alone is misleading on imbalanced problems: always predicting the majority class for a disease with 1 percent prevalence gives 99 percent accuracy and no value. The confusion matrix counts true positives, false positives, true negatives and false negatives. Precision is TP / (TP + FP), the fraction of predicted positives that are correct; recall is TP / (TP + FN), the fraction of actual positives that are found; the F1 score is their harmonic mean. The ROC curve plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate as the decision threshold varies, and the area under it, ROC AUC, equals the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative.

## Decision Trees and Ensembles

A decision tree predicts by asking a sequence of questions about the features, such as whether income exceeds a threshold, and following the matching branch until it reaches a leaf that holds a class or a value. Trees are learned greedily from the top down: at each node the algorithm picks the feature and threshold that best separate the training examples, then recurses into each child.

For classification, split quality is measured by impurity. Entropy, the sum of -p log2 p over the classes, is zero for a pure node and largest for an even mix; information gain is the drop in entropy from the parent to the weighted average of its children. Gini impurity, one minus the sum of squared class proportions, behaves similarly and is cheaper to compute, so CART uses it by default. Regression trees choose splits that minimise squared error.

A tree grown until every leaf is pure memorises the training set, so growth is limited by a maximum depth or a minimum number of samples per leaf, or the tree is pruned by cutting back branches that do not improve validation error. Single trees are easy to interpret but unstable: a small change in the data can produce a very different tree.

Ensembles combine many trees to fix this. Bagging, short for bootstrap aggregating, trains each tree on a bootstrap sample drawn with replacement and averages their predictions, which reduces variance. A random forest adds a second source of randomness: at each split only a random subset of the features is considered, commonly the square root of the number of features for classification. This decorrelates the trees, so averaging helps more. The examples left out of each bootstrap sample provide a free out-of-bag estimate of the error.

Boosting builds the ensemble sequentially, with each new weak learner concentrating on what the current ensemble gets wrong. AdaBoost reweights misclassified examples, while gradient boosting fits each new tree to the negative gradient of the loss, the residuals in the squared-error case, scaled by a small learning rate. XGBoost and LightGBM are optimised implementations of gradient boosted trees and are strong defaults for tabular data.

## Clustering with k-Means

Clustering groups unlabelled examples so that points in the same cluster are more similar to each other than to points in other clusters. It is the canonical unsupervised learning task: there are no labels, so the algorithm must find structure in the features alone.

The k-means algorithm partitions n points into k clusters, each represented by its centroid, the mean of its members. It minimises the within-cluster sum of squared distances, also called inertia. Lloyd's algorithm alternates two steps until nothing changes: the assignment step puts every point into the cluster of its nearest centroid, and the update step moves each centroid to the mean of the points assigned to it. Neither step can increase the objective, so the procedure always converges, but only to a local minimum that depends on where the centroids started.

Initialisation therefore matters. Random initialisation can place two centroids inside one natural cluster and none in another. The k-means++ scheme picks the first centroid uniformly at random and each later one with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest centroid already chosen, which spreads the seeds out and comes with a provable approximation guarantee. Running the algorithm several times and keeping the result with the lowest inertia is standard practice.

The number of clusters k must be chosen by the user. The elbow method plots inertia against k and looks for the point where adding clusters stops helping much, and the silhouette score compares each point's mean distance to its own cluster with its distance to the nearest other cluster.

Because k-means relies on Euclidean distances and means, it assumes roughly spherical clusters of similar size and is sensitive to feature scale and to outliers, so features are usually standardised first. Elongated or nested shapes are better handled by density-based methods such as DBSCAN, or by Gaussian mixture models fitted with expectation-maximisation, which behave like a soft, probabilistic version of k-means. High-dimensional data is often reduced first with principal component analysis, PCA, which projects it onto the directions of greatest variance.
